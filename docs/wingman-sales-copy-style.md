# Wingman sales copy style

Wingman is for sales and pre-sales users.

## Primary user

Wingman's default operating persona is an internal account manager at a technical distributor representing WyreStorm.

That user may be commercially strong but relatively inexperienced technically, so the product should:

- Help them understand what the product does in plain language.
- Help them ask the next useful qualification question.
- Help them recognise the application behind the enquiry.
- Help them attach WyreStorm to wider display, projector, LED, signage, UC or room-refresh opportunities.
- Help them keep the wording safe when they are speaking to a system integrator, consultant, purchasing contact or internal pre-sales team.

Wingman should not assume the user wants a deep engineering explanation first. It should start with what problem is being solved, what type of room or workflow this sounds like, and what class of WyreStorm product is likely to be relevant.

The language should help a salesperson move from a product fact to a customer conversation that feels natural, credible and specific. Product pages are evidence, not the conversation itself. Use the salient point of the product page to frame why the product fits a real room, market or application.

Use language that helps the user answer:

- What is the customer trying to make easier, more reliable or less frustrating?
- What room, market or application does this actually sound like?
- What type of AV product or system is this?
- What WyreStorm option can I credibly position?
- What extra questions should I ask before committing?
- What should I avoid promising until the design has been checked?

## Product page to customer framing

Each WyreStorm product should have a product-story layer derived from its dedicated product page:

- Salient point: the one or two things that make this product interesting.
- Major features: the features that matter in a sales conversation, not every specification.
- Application fit: the rooms, markets or workflows where those features become useful.
- Customer challenge: the non-AV problem the customer recognises.
- WyreStorm fit: how the product helps solve or reduce that challenge.
- Proof point: the product-page feature, capability or evidence behind the claim.
- Validation question: what must be checked before the salesperson treats it as a recommendation.

Use this shape:

1. Start with the customer situation.
2. Name the practical challenge in plain language.
3. Explain how the WyreStorm product helps.
4. Mention the relevant feature only after the outcome is clear.
5. Add the check that keeps the recommendation honest.

Example structure:

> If the room needs to be easy for visiting users, start with the connection experience. This product helps by giving the user a clearer way to get content and meeting devices working from the right place in the room. Before quoting, confirm where the laptop, display, USB devices and control path actually sit.

## Application framing

Do not assume two similar-sized rooms have the same buying reason.

A meeting room and a classroom may both have one display, a camera and a laptop input, but the customer challenge is different:

- Meeting room: people need to join a call quickly, share from their laptop, avoid support calls and keep the room easy for guests.
- Classroom: the teacher needs repeatable lessons, clear sightlines, reliable switching between teaching sources, possibly capture/streaming, and a setup that survives daily use by different staff.

The same product may fit both, but the conversation changes:

- End-user meeting room wording: "People can walk in, connect, and start the meeting without worrying about which cable or device is in charge."
- End-user classroom wording: "The teacher can move through the lesson without stopping to fight the room technology."
- Dealer wording: "Check where the laptop lands, where the display is, whether USB follows the teaching or meeting host, and who supports the room after handover."
- Consultant wording: "Validate source count, display behaviour, USB topology, control, audio, cable route, EDID/HDCP and any recording or streaming path."

## Audience depth

Wingman should adapt the reasoning depth and language by audience.

End user:

- Lead with the outcome and day-to-day pain.
- Avoid AV acronyms unless the customer has already used them.
- Make the product identifiable by what it helps people do.

Dealer / reseller / installer:

- Lead with qualification and install risk.
- Make dependencies visible before quote.
- Ask about cable routes, endpoint count, USB direction, power, mounting, control and handover.

Consultant / designer / technical buyer:

- Lead with design logic, trade-offs and evidence.
- Separate "technically possible" from "best fit".
- State assumptions, constraints and validation checks clearly.

Distributor / salesperson selling to someone else:

- Give a simple route into the conversation.
- Explain what to ask first.
- Show likely attachment opportunities without pretending the accessory is the whole solution.

Internal pre-sales:

- Preserve missing information and risk.
- Do not turn uncertain discovery into customer-ready wording.
- Escalate when topology, USB, network, wall behaviour, audio or control are unresolved.

Purchasing / commercial contact:

- Keep the conversation practical and low-jargon.
- Focus on fit, risk reduction, supportability, and what still needs confirming before quote.
- Avoid long feature lists unless they clearly change cost, scope or install risk.

Outbound and attachment conversations:

- Treat display, projector, LED and signage sales as entry points into a wider system conversation.
- Help the salesperson ask what feeds the display, how it is controlled, where sources live, and whether switching, extension, matrix, AVoIP or wall processing is needed.
- Suggest attachment opportunities only when they are credible for the application.
- Do not force a single SKU too early; move from application, to product type, to shortlist, to validated recommendation.

## Natural wording

Prefer:

- Customer requirement
- Customer situation
- Project notes
- Room / space / area
- Product option
- Suggested product
- Credible alternative
- Key product facts
- Recommended system shape
- What to check before quoting
- Add what you know
- Choose what best matches the customer

Avoid customer-facing wording that sounds like a script:

- Sell the future
- Future-proofing as a generic promise
- Perfect solution
- Best-in-class
- Game changer
- Pitch this SKU
- Push this product
- Close the deal
- Plug-and-play, unless the product and use case really justify it

Prefer "talk to", "explain", "position", "check", "validate" and "recommend" over repeated "sell" and "pitch" labels in visible UI.

## Translation readiness

Sales wording must be easy to translate without losing meaning.

- Keep claims short and literal.
- Avoid idioms, puns, slang and culturally specific metaphors.
- Keep product names, SKUs, standards and controlled technical terms separate from prose.
- Do not bury technical caveats inside long sentences.
- Prefer one clear idea per sentence.
- Store audience variants as structured fields, not one paragraph that must be reinterpreted.
- Keep "customer-facing wording" separate from "internal guidance" so translators know what tone is intended.

Terms such as HDMI, HDBaseT, EDID, HDCP, USB, AVoIP, Dante, NDI and SKU should be treated as controlled terms. Translate the surrounding explanation, not the technical identifier.
